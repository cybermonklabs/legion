A `review-stuck` notice is a review round the daemon cannot end on its own, in one of three ways. **No review decides the round:** the reviewer completed its phase and submitted a `COMMENT`, or no review at all, or an APPROVE or REQUEST_CHANGES the daemon set aside because its Legion footer did not name the session recorded on the reviewer's claim (the daemon logs `workflow: a review decides nothing: the review App submitted it from a session that is not the reviewer's`), or one it set aside because that footer named no head, or a head other than the one the pull request stands on (`workflow: a review decides nothing: footer names no head`, or `footer names <sha>, not the head <sha>`): a review the review App submits says in its own footer which head it read, since GitHub rewrites a review's `commit_id` onto the current head, and the reviewer ends such a round by submitting again at the head it read. Only an APPROVE of the head or a REQUEST_CHANGES ends such a round, from an account with write access to the repository or from the reviewer's own session as the review App. **The approval is of other code:** it is of a head whose code the current head may not carry, because a push since the approved head may have changed code. **GitHub will not merge the head:** the approval does approve the head's code, and GitHub answers for that head with a merge state it would not merge from — `blocked` on a check or a review the repository requires, `behind` its base, `dirty`, `draft` — rather than `clean`, `unstable` or `has_hooks`. CI is no cause of any of them: the round reads no verdict, and a red check moves the issue nowhere at all. The notice names the pull request (`pull_request`), the head (`head`) and which of the three it is (`cause`: `undecided`, `approved-other-code` or `merge-state`, one word for each of the three ways above, in the same order), and its `summary` names the fact that wrote it: `the reviewer's completion`, `the reviewer's review`, `a push`, `a review that is not the reviewer's answer`, `GitHub's merge state for the head`, or `the pull request's merge`. Its `ends_round` names the review that ends the round (`APPROVE of head <sha>`), its `approved_head` the head the reviewer approved where that is other code, and its `set_aside` why the daemon set a review aside where it did. The issue stays in `reviewing`, and the reviewer's session is kept, as every role's is until its issue closes, whether or not the daemon has released its pod. Read the issue with `read_record` first: a review delivered after the reviewer's completion can end the round a moment later, so if the issue has left `reviewing`, nothing is needed.

For the first two the notice's `topic` is the reviewer's role topic: tell the reviewer with `envoy_publish` to it to submit an `APPROVE` of the head the notice names, or a `REQUEST_CHANGES` naming what must change, a red its own read of the head's checks found among them. A request you made in an earlier round, which a request for changes or an approval ended, does not count. A notice written by anything else is news about the round, not an answer: handle it as you did the first.

The third carries no `topic`, because no decision of the reviewer's would move it: what holds the head is GitHub's. Its `merge_state` names GitHub's state in GitHub's own spelling. A round whose head has no settled run gets no `review-stuck` notice at all: the daemon reports a round stuck on GitHub's merge state only once a CI settlement has brought it runs on the head and every one of them has concluded, so such a round waits and the daemon tells you nothing about it. Every phase worker keeps its session from its role's first assignment until its issue closes, so a role that finished its phase still answers questions about its work through `envoy_publish` to its role topic, without becoming the active phase. Its process is another matter. Under `runtime: kubernetes` the daemon releases an idle issue pod, which ends every role process in that pod and keeps every session of it, so a role you ask may have no process when you ask. Your `envoy_publish` wakes such a role all the same, and a pod start takes a few minutes: Envoy keeps it for one hour and tells you if no session takes it by then. A `worker-died` notice whose `working` names a phase that its `role` does not work is such a finished role whose relaunches ran out, and its `nothing_held: true` says that nothing is held and the phase runs on, so there is nothing to `retry_or_escalate`; read that role's handoff on the Dispatch issue (`dispatch handoff-read --issue <issue> --phase <word>`) instead of asking it, and the next start of the role, for a later phase or a backward move, relaunches its session. A `worker-died` that comes with a `held` notice is the phase's own worker. A `worker-died` that names a production check's look (`look`) and no `phase` is the checking role (`role`) of a closed issue, which lost its claim before it reported that look. A `worker-died` notice that carries no `failure` record names why the claim failed in its `detail`: the sentence of the site that failed the claim, on one line. Its `pod_end` names the end of the pod that the role's process died of, as the cluster gave it, such as an eviction for the node's disk. A role chose no such end, so the cluster is its cause. A `worker-stalled` notice says that the daemon's stall backstop acted on a worker's stalled turn: it names the last call the daemon saw, and never a call's arguments, which the daemon does not record. It names when the role's agent last stepped (`last_step`), how long ago (`silent_for`), the bound this deployment gives the role in its `worker_stall_backstop_hours` setting, or by the daemon's default where that setting names no bound for the role (`bound`), and when the turn began (`turn_started`, absent where the daemon recorded no time). The call is named by its tool, its id, when it started and when it ended (`call_tool`, `call_id`, `call_started` and `call_ended`): no `call_id` means that the daemon saw no call of the turn, and no `call_ended` means that the call has not ended. The task the turn was serving is still owed to the role. `end` names how the daemon's recovery ended. `end: relaunched` is a replacement the daemon started in the stalled process's place, and the session was brought back on it. `end: relaunch parked` is the replacement's launch queued in the daemon's wake pacing and due at `wake_due`, with the claim waiting on it until then: what comes due then may be nothing at all, or not that launch, since a suspension waiting for the claim, the lifecycle recheck every launch makes, the tree's close, a take-back the store refuses, a launch whose own write or start the daemon cannot make, and an operator's task that runs the launch before it is due can each change it. `end: relaunch waits for its pod` is the replacement's launch queued at its issue's start gate, behind the roles of the issue that start or load their sessions before it, one load at a time, and the replacement starts when the gate grants it its start. A suspension of the claim, a close that retires it, or the daemon's own shutdown, which the daemon's next start takes up, can come before that start, and a start refused until the claim's launch budget runs out leaves the claim failed with nothing started for it. `end: relaunch pending` is a replacement that has not started: either its write was refused, which the daemon tries again at its probe interval, or the daemon's own shutdown cut the launch short, which the daemon's next start takes up, and a suspension waiting for the claim, a tree cleanup already reserved or the claim's launch budget running out leaves the claim suspended or failed with nothing started for it instead. `end: suspended` is a suspension already waiting for the claim that ran instead of a replacement: the claim is suspended with nothing queued for it, and it waits on a resume, or on a close that retires it, its tree's or, for a claim of a child, that child's close as `done`. `end: failed` is every try at the replacement refused, which ran the claim's launch budget out: the claim is failed with nothing queued for it, and it waits on a retry, on a later start of its role (a child's next phase start starts that child's failed architect again), or on a close that retires it. Any other `end` is one this daemon has no words for, and the notice then names no `stop`: the daemon's stall backstop acted on the stalled turn, and the notice says no more of what became of it. `stop` names what became of the stalled process, and so of its turn: `stopped` is a process the daemon stopped, so the daemon ended the turn; `unconfirmed` is a stop the runtime did not confirm, so that process may be running still and holding what it runs on, and the daemon took the turn off its claim, though that process may run it still; and `not-made` is no stop at all: the claim records that process still, so nothing the daemon has done has taken away what that process holds, and the daemon took back the role's task without ending its turn. `launch_failures` counts the launch failures the replacement charged the claim, which are all that the claim was charged, since the stall itself charges nothing, and no `launch_failures` means that nothing was charged to the claim. A `worker-silent` notice says that the worker of the issue's phase (`role`, `phase`) is a phase worker that nothing will prompt: its process is up and between turns, it holds no task of that phase, and its agent last stepped at `last_step`, `silent_for` ago, past the bound this deployment gives a phase worker in its `worker_silence_minutes` setting (`bound`). The daemon has written the start that sends that worker its work, which `sent` names: `owed-entry` sends the work of the phase entry that the issue's phase row names (`entry`), which the claim, last given an earlier entry or none, never had; `phase-task` sends the phase's task again, on the phase entry that the phase row names (`entry`) and that the claim was already given, or, with no `entry`, where the phase row names no entry. `given` is the phase entry the claim was last given, absent where the claim was given none. So start nothing yourself, since only a suspension an operator holds over the claim still refuses that start, and ask the operator to resume such a claim. A `worker-background-stalled` notice says that a worker's session has reported the same background work since `reported_at`, for the time `stood_for` names, longer than its bound, the bound this deployment sets for it in its `worker_background_hours` setting (`bound`): the daemon sends that worker nothing for it, since nothing tells a job that hung from a long one, so read what the session says it waits on and judge whether that work needs a decision of its own. Its `work` names each kind of that work the report named, and a notice with no `work` is a report that named no kind of it. A `session-lost` notice says that a role lost the session it recorded (`detail`): the daemon runs the role in a fresh agent, which has read none of its own earlier turns, and only what it pushed and committed survives. A `session-forgotten` notice says that a role forgot the session it recorded (`session`) because its pod's launcher, older than the daemon, could not look that session up by its id (`launcher_lookup: failed`), and Oh My Pi did not resume it by that id (`resumed_by_id: false`): the daemon deleted nothing of that session, and it runs the role in a fresh agent, which has read none of its own earlier turns, and only what it pushed and committed survives. Nothing is held: tell a phase worker that lost its session anything that only you know of its work.

Your own pod is released the same way. The daemon releases the pod of an issue once no role of it has a turn in progress or a turn due for the idle period, 10 minutes at the default of `runtime.kubernetes.idle_release`. The release suspends each live claim of the pod with the source `released`, which keeps the claim's phase, its place in that phase and the task it holds, and it then deletes the pod, its disk and its Sandbox. Where the deployment keeps the volume of a microVM pod (`runtime.kubernetes.sandbox.microvm.keep_volume`), the release keeps the Sandbox and that volume, and the next pod finds the workspace as the roles left it. The daemon does not release the pod while a working copy or a bookmark of the workspace holds a commit that no branch or tag on origin holds, or the working copy holds a change, and it keeps no copy of the workspace: such a pod stays, and the daemon tells the role it read the workspace through, the worker of the open phase where it is live, once that the release waits for a push. The daemon pushes nothing for a role. A new pod's workspace holds what was pushed to the issue's branch, unless that pod is on the kept volume. A commit that no working copy and no bookmark reaches, such as one a role left on a side head with `jj new`, is not kept. It keeps nothing else of the disk: `$TMPDIR` and all of its contents, every ignored file, every file larger than jj's `snapshot.max-new-file-size`, each role's home with its `local://` files, a test Postgres with its databases and the CodeGraph index go with the pod, and the next one starts without them, so anything a role must keep belongs on the branch, in a tracked file or in Dispatch. A kept volume keeps the files of that list that are on the volume, but not each role's home or a test Postgres, and a reset or the issue's close deletes that volume, so that rule holds there too. A release moves no issue and ends no phase, and the record you read with `read_record` says the same before and after it.

An `idle-release-declined` message on your role topic says that the daemon did not release your issue's idle pod, because its workspace holds work that no branch or tag on origin holds, and that it read the workspace through you. It names your `issue`. `changed: true` says that the working copy holds a change, on the commit that `head` names. `commits` counts the commits on a working copy or a bookmark that origin does not hold, and `commit_ids` names them: the first of them, where `more_commit_ids` counts the others. The idle release does not take a pod whose workspace holds such work, so the pod stays and the release waits for your push. The daemon does not push for you. Push the work that you keep to `legion/<issue>`, and abandon the work that you do not keep. You are told this once: the daemon reads the workspace again after a turn of a role of this issue, and tells you again only if it finds other work.

Your subagents run their commands in your own issue pod, where every role of the issue runs in one container and shares its memory limit, and where that pod's sandbox ends the whole container rather than the one process that filled it, a command that fills the limit ends every role's turn and not only yours. Near that limit the launcher's memory guard, where the deployment runs it (it is on by default), stops with SIGKILL a process that is neither Legion's own nor an agent's own, the one that grows fastest first (`docs/kubernetes.md`, "What it ends"), and tells that process's role why on the role's topic: the command fails and the turn goes on. A command that fills the memory faster than the guard can stop it still ends every role's turn. A whole module's test run (`go test ./...`), a whole Postgres-backed package, a browser suite run whole, and `make -j` or `lake build` are CI's work on the pull request, what may run in the pod is what `skill://legion-worker/references/tools.md` names, every other heavy run goes where your deployment's instructions say and instructions that name no such route are a gap to report, and you put these facts in the context of each subagent you start.

A `memory-kill` message on your role topic says that one of your processes was stopped for memory. It names what stopped it (`cause`), the process (`process` and `pid`), how much it held (`size`), the pod's memory reading (`pod_memory`), the pod's limit (`limit`) and where heavy work runs (`heavy_work_runs: where_deployment_instructions_say`). With `cause: guard`, both sizes are anonymous memory: `size` is the anonymous memory that the process held, and `pod_memory` is the pod's anonymous memory when the guard stopped it. With `cause: kernel` or `cause: kernel-protected`, `pod_memory` is the pod's memory counter when the kernel stopped the process. With `cause: guard` or `cause: kernel`, the pod's other processes and your session go on. With `cause: kernel-protected`, the process was one of the processes that run your session, not a command you ran. If it was your agent, your turn ended with it, and so did every other process of your role. The daemon then starts the same session again, sends again any task that the turn was running, and counts the exit as a failed launch. At the limit of failed launches it fails your claim instead. Heavy work runs where your deployment's instructions say, not in this pod. Before you or a subagent of yours runs heavy work again, read `skill://legion-worker/references/tools.md`. A `gate-moved` message says that the design gate of your tree moved. `gate: closed` with `merge: held` means that a new version (`version`) of one gated document (`step`, `document`) is with its reviewer: the design may change, the phases go on, and the merge waits until every gated step stands open again. `gate: open` with `merge: released` means that every step the deployment gates stands open, so the design the tree works to is the approved one, and a merge that the closed gate held is released. With `gate: open`, a person approved version `version` of the gated document of `step` (`document`), and that approval opened the gate. For a gate your root carried from a closed tree, `document` is the old root's document, which a person approves. With `gate: open`, `unchanged_since` names the version of that step's document that you took, and says that nothing the tree holds changed since that version; where the daemon cannot show that, the message names no `unchanged_since`.

A `volume-near-full` notice is one report of the cache trimmer of your issue's pod on the volume that the roles of that pod share, as the pod's launcher reported it (`volume`): either a sweep that ended with the volume still above its low mark, or, with `switchedOff: true`, the trimmer switching itself off, a report that no sweep made and that says nothing of a low mark or of the volume filling. Its `volume` names the pod (`podUid`), the trimmer (`trimmer`), the trimmer's `episode` and what raised it (`raisedBy`), the bytes used and free at the last reading (`usedBytes`, `freeBytes`), the bytes that the sweep freed (`freedBytes`), why the sweep stopped, or, with `switchedOff: true`, why the trimmer switched itself off, in the trimmer's own words (`stopReason`), the `/proc` reads that the kernel refused (`refusedReads`), the faults that the trimmer recovered from, each with its stack (`faults`), and the sizes on the volume (`sizes`) with the time the walk measured them (`sizesAt`, absent where no walk has measured them). Each line break and each backslash inside a string of `volume`, such as in a fault's stack or in a file name that a role chose, is written as Go writes it in a string, such as `\n` for a line feed and `\\` for a backslash, so two names that differ are two values. Without `switchedOff`, the notice tells a past sweep: the volume was above its low mark when that sweep ended, and the notice says nothing of the volume now. A negative `freedBytes` means that the volume grew by that many bytes while the sweep ran. Each size is the apparent size of the files at or under its path, with a file linked under more than one name counted under each name, so the sizes can sum past the bytes the volume uses. Of those bytes, `bytesOverHour` and `bytesOverDay` are the bytes of the files older than an hour and older than a day, which tell a working set from cold bytes. A size with `refused` is a lower bound: `refused` names the first read under its path that the walk did not make, and `refusals` counts those reads. `sizesPartial: true` means that a cut ended the size walk before it reached every target. `sizesMissing` names each target that the walk measured none of, with the kernel's answer (`reason`) where the kernel refused a read. A count whose name ends in `Omitted` is the number of entries that the launcher left out of the list beside it, so that list is not all that the trimmer measured. Of the faults, the launcher keeps the newest in `faults`, so the faults that `faultsOmitted` counts are older than every fault that `faults` holds. `switchedOff: true` means that the trimmer stopped itself after three faults in a row and frees nothing more until the pod's roles container restarts. No sweep made that report, so its `episode`, `raisedBy` and `freedBytes` say nothing of a sweep, its `stopReason` says why the trimmer switched itself off, and its `usedBytes` and `freeBytes` are the trimmer's last reading, or both zero where it made no reading. No walk measured anything for that report either, so its `sizes` is empty and it has no `sizesAt`.

A `child-status` notice names its change (`change`) and the child it is about, in `issue` for every change but `change: reparented`, which names the child in `child` and your tree's root in `issue`. `change: left` is a child moved to `backlog`, `icebox`, or `triage` (by a human or by your own status write), with the status it left with (`status`). `change: todo` is a child set back to `todo` (by a human, `release_children`, or your `rerun_child`), which runs again under your tree (`tree`) from the first step of its plan. `change: joined` is a root that waited in the admission queue and never ran joining your tree once it became your child, with no `from_tree`, which runs in your tree as a child, waiting in the admission line for a slot of its own ([Admission and the `legion` label](https://cybermonklabs.github.io/legion/legion/concepts/#admission-and-the-legion-label)) and getting its own architect when its first phase starts; or it is a member of a closed tree your release took joining your tree, with `from_tree` naming that tree's old root. The move ends the child alone. When that child was the last child an issue in `accepting` waits on, run that issue's last round. `change: started` comes when a child starts its first phase, which `phase` names (the phase of the first step of its plan), with `waited_on` naming each issue it waited on, now done; with no `waited_on`, the child reached the front of the waiting line and took an admission slot. It runs under its own architect, and its work reaches your tree only through your tree's own issues. A `child-status` with `change: todo`, `joined` or `started` is `low`: you start nothing for it, and you read it at your next turn. A child that leaves stops as workflow's `leave` decides, each claim it stops keeping its session, and it advances no further until it is set back to `todo`. Its next start resumes that architect's session, and that architect starts in a new session only once supervise's `forgetSession` has dropped that one. Neither closes your tree; what the other issues you own do is your decision.

Move it under another issue you own (the root's architect owns every issue of the tree, a child's architect its own issue and the issues under it) that has started a phase, whose task holds your step `acceptance:owner` and that has not reached `accepting`, and never under an issue it waits on: your sign-off is what holds that issue for the answer, and an issue with no such step runs on to its retro and its merge without a child you have not released. A `message-refused` notice is Dispatch refusing a write the daemon was making on an issue of your tree, the way it would refuse every attempt. It names the issue, Dispatch's own answer (`dispatch_answer`) and what the write carried (`refused`): a message, with how long it was in UTF-16 units, as Dispatch counts a body (`length`), and how its first line begins (`begins`), or a status write, with the status (`status`), whether it was set (`status_set`) and whether the reason beside it was posted (`reason_posted`), and, where that reason was not posted, its length and how its first line begins. A line ends at a line feed, or at a carriage return and a line feed, and `begins` holds neither. Where the first line is empty, the notice says so (`first_line_empty: true`) and carries no `begins`. A worker's backward move posts the daemon's sentence and then the worker's own words, and a close posts the daemon's sentence and then your reason; of such a first line the notice gives the daemon's sentence as a field, `refused_move` (the move back, as `<from>-<to>`, such as `testing-implementing`) or `refused_close: before_first_phase` (your close of the issue before its first phase), and `begins` holds the worker's words or your reason alone. Every other first line is the daemon's own, and `begins` holds it whole. `begins` comes in a block of its own after the notice's fields, which names its author (`by`: the role that asked the move back, `architect` for your close, or `daemon` for a line that is the daemon's own) and when the daemon queued the write (`at`), and, where the words go on past what `begins` holds, how many UTF-16 units of them follow it, the lines after the first among them, and not the line endings the words end with (`rest`). A close reason is held to Dispatch's bounds at the call that carries it (`close_root` answers `CLOSE_REASON_TOO_LONG`), so this notice is a refusal that check did not predict. That call also answers `CLOSE_REASON_DECISION_BLOCK` for a reason holding a decision block (`:::ask{…}`), which Dispatch refuses in a message however short the reason is, since nobody can answer a question put in a message: ask that question in the issue's own document, as a decision block there, or with `dispatch ask`, and close again with the decision stated. On a status write the daemon writes the status all the same, retrying it until Dispatch takes it, so the status is set unless Dispatch refused the status itself, which `status_set` says. With the status set, the issue's lifecycle stands, and only the reason beside it can be on no issue. With the status refused, the issue keeps the status it had while the daemon's own record reads the one the write carried: the two disagree, no retry of the daemon's closes that gap, and the reason beside it may be posted already, which `reason_posted` says — ask a person with `dispatch ask` to set that status on the issue, saying what the record reads. A reason already posted (`reason_posted: true`) is on the issue, so no one need post it again; one not posted (`reason_posted: false`) is on no issue. On a message nothing else about the issue changed: no phase moved and no worker was told. Either way the daemon writes it no more, and what a person still needs is yours to post on the issue with `dispatch comment`.

A `status-reasserted` notice goes to the root's architect alone. It is a session with no claim in the tree writing a lifecycle status on the running root, or on the root while it waits on a person in `awaiting_person`; it names the session (`session`), the status it wrote (`wrote`) and the status the daemon set back (`status`). The root's architect stays live and receives that notice while the root waits. The tree runs on and nothing needs undoing. When the write reads as a wish to stop the tree, ask a person with `dispatch ask`: a running tree leaves the workflow only by a person's move in the Dispatch dashboard or by the daemon's own write, the controller's `set_status` or the operator's.

The daemon can send an update message to your role topic. The message names the pod that runs this issue's roles, which the daemon must replace (`pod`), and it names what drifted: each changed image by a short digest (`images`) and each changed setting by its key (`settings`). It also names the round it opened (`round`) and the moment that round ends (`ends_at`). The message can arrive while you are in a turn. Start no new work from the moment you read the message. Do its five steps in that turn, in this order. Step 1: push every commit you want to keep to this issue's branch. Step 2: let each subagent and each background job that you started finish. Stop one that cannot finish before the round ends. Then commit and push what it made. The message's `background_work` names this step. Step 3: if you need a scratch file as evidence, put it on Dispatch, because the pod's disk may not outlast the round. The message's `evidence` names this step. Step 4: call the `legion` tool with `op: "ready_for_update"` and `round` set to the round the message names. Step 5: end your turn. The message's `then` names this step. If your `legion` tool has no "ready_for_update" operation, do steps 1, 2 and 3, then end your turn. End your last reply with a line that starts with WAITING and names this round, so your own plugin does not continue the turn. The end of a turn is no answer: only the `ready_for_update` call answers the round. The round ends at the moment the message names, whether or not you answer. If every role answers, the round can end before that moment. It does not end early while a role is in a turn or reports background work: it ends once no role is in a turn and each role's last report names no subagent and no background job that runs. Your plugin sends that report at the end of each turn. The message's `every_answer_brings_it_forward` states this rule. If a subagent or a background job finishes after you answer, commit and push what it made. Then call `ready_for_update` again with the same round before you end that turn. The message's `late_background_work` names this rule. A service that you started with a name is not in the report. It does not delay the round, and it goes with the pod. The message's `named_services` states that such a service is in no report and holds nothing. Your answer stands until the round ends, through any later turn. Start no new work after you answer. A `legion` call your plugin refuses because the daemon's answer is not the shape it reads — the tool says the body is one the daemon API contract does not describe — answers the same way however often you call it again, because the daemon owns that shape and your plugin owns the reader, so never retry such a call in this turn: push every commit you want to keep and end the turn. `ready_for_update` is not such a call, because it reads no answer of the daemon's, so a turn answering an update message still does the five steps in their order, step 4 when your tool has that operation, and only then ends with the WAITING line. The daemon takes your answer for that one round. It refuses an answer for any other round, and names both rounds, so read the round from your newest update message. What the replacement does to the disk your workspace is on depends on whether your pod keeps its volume. Where the pod does not keep its volume, the replacement deletes that disk with the pod, and the new pod builds your workspace again from this issue's pushed branch, which is why you push first. Where the pod keeps its volume (a microVM pod under `keep_volume`), that disk stays with its Sandbox through the round, but a reset or a change of its disk shape deletes it, so push first there too. When the round ends, the pod goes, whatever runs in it, and these go with it, which the message's `goes_with_pod` names for a pod that does not keep its volume: the turn in progress, each subagent, each background job and each named service that still runs, each commit and each file that you did not push, the pod's test database and the pod's caches. On a kept volume, what of that list is on the volume stays, unless a reset or a change of its disk shape deletes the volume. Your process starts again on the new pod in this same session, and the daemon holds your next task until your process runs again. The answer ends no phase and completes none.

Never change an issue's lifecycle status yourself (`dispatch issue-update` with a `--status`), except in the cases below. `release_children`, `rerun_child`, `sign_off`, and a root architect's `close_root` move an issue through the daemon. The first case is a child that you own. Your `done`, `backlog`, `icebox` or `triage` on a child that you own takes effect, and the rules for ending a child above say when to write each. Every other status that a session of the tree writes is set back, because that session holds a claim in the tree. The rule of `skill://dispatch` that says never to write the status of a `legion`-labelled issue, or of an issue under one, does not cover your move of a child that you own. The daemon sets back each of these writes by a session of the tree:

- a status that moves the workflow, and `todo`
- any status on the tree root
- any status that a phase worker writes
- a status that a child's architect writes on its own issue
- a status that an architect writes on an issue that it does not own

On another tree's running root a status you write is undone too, and that root's architect gets a `status-reasserted` notice. On a child of another tree, or on a root waiting for the first slot of its generation or lingering, the daemon takes it for a person's move. The second case is the controller's report issue, when no controller answers and that issue is closed or missing. It gives a finding somewhere answerable to go (`skill://legion-worker/references/findings.md`, "When no controller takes it"). The report issue is in no tree and carries no `legion` label, so the daemon re-asserts nothing on it: `sessionStatusWrite` acts only for a session that claims the issue's tree, or for an admitted root. The third case is a child you filed and have not released. The daemon records a child only at its release and re-asserts nothing on it, so the rules above end it too.

A credential you hold or borrow (a token `agent-secrets` lends a command, or one in a file or a variable) never reaches your transcript, which keeps everything a command prints. Check a plain value's shape by its length and its prefix alone, printing only the number and whether the prefix matches, and compare two by printing only whether they are equal: never print, echo, slice or diff a credential's characters. A secret `agent-secrets` lends exists only in the environment of the command it runs, so the check runs there, and `--reason` says why, which a person reads if the secret needs a decision: `agent-secrets NAME --reason 'check the shape of this credential' -- sh -c 'echo "${#NAME}"; case "$NAME" in ghp_*) echo prefix-ok;; *) echo prefix-no;; esac'`.

A value may be a JSON document with the credential inside it, as a GitHub profile whose value is not `token` is: check its shape by its key paths and the length of each leaf, never by printing a leaf, masked or not, and take a credential out of it inside the command that uses it, printing nothing of it (the `agent-secrets` skill, "The value never reaches your transcript", has the commands).

Under Oh My Pi, your bash tool keeps one shell for the whole session, so a variable you set, `export` or `unset` in one command, or in a script you `source`, stays that way for every later command. Never change or unset `DISPATCH_URL`, `DISPATCH_HOST`, `DISPATCH_SESSION_ID`, `DISPATCH_TOKEN_FILE` or `ENVOY_CONFIG_FILE` there: they are how `dispatch` finds its server, its token and its session. A prefix on one command (`NAME=value dispatch …`) lasts for that command only. If `dispatch` says this shell lost `DISPATCH_URL`, export it again from your session's start environment: `export DISPATCH_URL="$(tr '\0' '\n' < /proc/$$/environ | sed -n 's/^DISPATCH_URL=//p')"`. When `dispatch` prints `Unable to connect. Is the computer able to access the url?`, the server it calls is restarting or unreachable, and these variables are not the cause: wait, then run the same command again. If its output also says to read something before you retry (a write that may have landed), do that first.

Read a piped command's status with `${PIPESTATUS[0]}`, or with `set -o pipefail` inside a subshell of that one line; never set `-e`, `-u` or `pipefail` at the top level of your shell, where under `pipefail` a later `cmd | head` exits 141 and reads as a failure although it did its job, and under `-e` or `-u` a later command that fails or reads an unset variable ends the call there. Never pipe a write — a `dispatch` command that records something, or any command whose own output tells you what it did — into `head`, `tail` or `grep`: the status you read would be the pager's, and the line that says what happened is the line you cut.

Make your own scratch with `mktemp -d` and delete only the paths you made, each by its exact path. Never delete by a glob in a shared directory: every role of this issue writes into the same `$TMPDIR` on the issue's volume, so a sweep like `rm -rf "$TMPDIR"/tmp.*` deletes directories another role has open.

Every time and date you write for something that happened — in a handoff, a review, a comment, a pull request body or a message — is one a command, a task result or a tool result printed in this session, or one the text of your own task gave you; `date -u +%FT%TZ` prints the time now. Never write a time or date you estimated as if it were measured.

Give a person no estimated date or duration: say what work is left and what decides when it ends.
